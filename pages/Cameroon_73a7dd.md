## Why Douala Became Cameroon’s UFO Anchor

Douala’s role was partly practical. As Cameroon’s largest city and commercial centre, it offered better internet access, more social networks, and stronger links to francophone media and diaspora communities than most other parts of the country. When the French-rooted association “Les Repas Ufologiques” announced a Cameroon branch in April 2016, the organisation chose Douala as its base and identified “Mathéo Mapan” as organiser. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lesrepasufologiques.org/category/cameroun/page/2/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lesrepasufologiques.org">[LES REPAS UFOLOGIQUES]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lesrepasufologiques.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">LES REPAS UFOLOGIQUESDouala (Cameroun) Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Page 2 sur 2Le repas-ufologique de Douala au Cameroun annonce sa première réunion qui aura lieu le 4 juillet 2016, derrière l&#x27;hôtel « Hil...</span></span></span>

The structure of the meetings also mattered. “Repas Ufologiques” events were not formal scientific conferences. They were discussion meals or informal gatherings where participants exchanged stories, debated interpretations, and compared experiences. That format lowered the barrier for participation in a setting where discussing UFOs publicly could easily attract ridicule or accusations of superstition. In Cameroon, where unexplained events are often interpreted through religious, spiritual, or ancestral frameworks, the meetings created a semi-public space where people could discuss anomalous experiences without immediately placing them inside church, occult, or political narratives.

The available records suggest the Douala group remained small. Attendance lists from July and August 2016 mention only a handful of participants. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lesrepasufologiques.org/category/cameroun/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lesrepasufologiques.org">[LES REPAS UFOLOGIQUES]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lesrepasufologiques.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">LES REPAS UFOLOGIQUESDouala (Cameroun) ArchivesPremière réunion des Repas Ufologiques de Douala au Cameroun. Instead, the Douala meetings resembled a fragile intellectual micro-community held together by personal enthusiasm rather than institutional support.

## Witness Memory in a Low-Archive Environment

Douala’s UFO culture highlights a broader issue within African ufology: many claims survive primarily through oral transmission rather than through institutional documentation.

In countries with active air forces, civilian aviation transparency, or large UFO research groups, investigators may compare witness statements against radar logs, weather data, astronomical records, or government files. Cameroon offers very little of that infrastructure publicly. As a result, memory itself becomes the archive.

That creates several problems:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Time distortion:** witnesses often recount events years later.
* **Story convergence:** repeated group discussion can gradually standardise memories.
* **Cultural reinterpretation:** unexplained lights may later be linked to spiritual or ancestral ideas.
* **Verification gaps:** many claims cannot be independently checked after the fact.

</div>

At the same time, dismissing all such testimony would overlook why these stories persist. In Douala, UFO discussion often functioned as a way of talking about uncertainty, modernity, technology, religion, and invisible power. The meetings reveal how people attempt to organise unusual experiences into narratives that feel socially meaningful.

This is why the Douala material is historically useful even when it is not scientifically decisive. It documents the social life of UFO belief and curiosity inside an under-documented national setting.

They are stronger than anonymous internet rumours because names, dates, and organisers are identifiable. Yet they remain far weaker than cases supported by technical documentation.

This distinction is especially important in Cameroon’s wider UFO history. Later Cameroon-related stories, including the Moloundou electricity-infrastructure claims discussed elsewhere in the national record, inherited some of the same evidential weaknesses: compelling local narratives combined with very limited independently verifiable data. Douala’s meetings effectively established the pattern.
